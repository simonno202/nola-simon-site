*Part 1 of the Made in Canada series, part of Nola Simon Leadership: [Everyday Futurism Practices](/leadership)*

I didn't set out to make a series about Canadian entrepreneurship.

I went back through my podcast archive and noticed that about two dozen of my conversations were with Canadian guests, most of them entrepreneurs, recorded between 2021 and 2025. Building from small towns, islands, big cities and tiny condos. Listened to one at a time, each is a conversation about a career, a business, an idea. Listened to together, they tell a different story.

They tell what it's actually like to build work independently in this country. They aren't success stories. Lived experience is what they hold, recorded while it was still happening.

That's the starting point for [Made in Canada: the future of work, built independently](https://podcasts.apple.com/ca/podcast/made-in-canada-made-in-canada-building-a-business/id1602822494?i=1000802966468), and for this series of essays.

## Why now

As Canada looks for ways to reduce our dependence on the U.S. and safeguard our sovereignty, how we work independently is part of the answer. The geopolitical ground under Canadian work is shifting. Many of the assumptions we built our careers and businesses on, about where our customers, platforms and partners come from, don't hold the way they used to.

So what can the last five years teach us about how people have actually been building and operating here?

More than I expected, honestly.

## What my guests saw early

Listening back, I kept stopping the playback. My guests were naming things most people saw later. AI making confident mistakes about real people. Hiring algorithms no one was auditing. Return-to-office mandates built on distrust. Monitoring software that keeps people glued to their screens. Commitments to diversity, equity and inclusion that disappeared with the first round of layoffs.

What they were doing was paying attention to what was already in front of them, and saying it out loud.

That's what I mean by [everyday futurism](/everyday-futurism). The future doesn't arrive as a forecast. It shows up in ordinary conversations, early, before anyone has officially named it.

## The thread is trust

Almost every conversation comes back to trust. Who we trust with our data, our careers and our story, and whether that trust has been earned. The trust signals we send when we're not in the room. The ones we leave online for algorithms to read.

And underneath that, how much we trust ourselves.

Every Canadian guest in this archive bet on themselves. Some started a business. Some moved home. Some built from a small town, or rethought what independent work could look like here. That's self-trust before anyone handed them permission, and it's a skill the future of work is going to demand of all of us.

## Listening as a practice

Here's the part I didn't anticipate. Re-listening turned out to be a foresight practice in its own right.

The stories we tell about ourselves are foundational, but we're always reinterpreting them. Coming back to a conversation years later, you hear it differently. Some of it feels like history. Some of it still feels current. Some of it looks prescient. And some of it has quietly changed, for my guests and for me. Even the name of this podcast has changed three times.

That sorting is the skill. Noticing which assumptions still hold. Deciding what's worth carrying forward and what you can let go. Making sense of change while it's still happening, not after someone has written the case study.

Podcasts are good for this because they capture people mid-thought. Nobody is presenting a finished strategy. You hear the hesitation, the half-formed idea, the moment someone says the thing they haven't said publicly yet. That's where the signals are.

They're also accessible in a way many other mediums aren't. You can listen on any player, read a transcript, watch on YouTube, download an episode for later or get notified by email when a new one drops. The reach is global, anywhere you can connect. In that way, podcasts are a lot like radio. Anyone can tune in, and the learning travels with you.

## From listening to action

At the end of the day, only you know what you've learned from a podcast. Nobody grades it. And listening without action is just entertainment.

So how do you take the ideas and shape real-life behaviour? Start small. Write down the one idea that stuck with you, the assumption it made you question and one thing you'll do differently this week. Then notice what happens when you try it. That's the gap between consuming ideas and practising them, and it's the part only you can do.

## Try it yourself

Think of it as Choose Your Own Adventure meets Mission: Impossible. Your mission, should you choose to accept it: how can an archive of interviews with Canadians across the country help us build the future of work in Canada and safeguard our sovereignty?

I made a listening guide so you can practise this with any episode in the series. The [Everyday Futurism Listening Guide](/made-in-canada-listening-guide.pdf) is a fillable PDF. It asks what feels like history, what still feels current and what's worth carrying forward, and it leaves space for the best question each episode asks, or makes you ask.

If you want to go further, look up what the guests are doing now. I know where they've gone, but it isn't my place to tell their stories for them. See whether you can hear, in the conversation, where they were heading and what they later let go.

The past, the present and the future are always telling us something. The work is in how we make sense of it.

So what are you building on that you'd never questioned until now?
